Field guide notes for Nymphalidae · Charaxinae (Rajahs & Nawabs)

Family overview (Nymphalidae, Brush-footed butterflies): Tamil Nadu’s largest butterfly family (97 checklist species), encompassing tigers, crows, pansies, eggflies, sailers, barons, rajahs, browns, and Tamil Nadu’s state butterfly, the Tamil Yeoman.

Subfamily traits (Charaxinae): Stout-bodied, exceptionally fast-flying canopy butterflies often attracted to tree sap, rotting fruit, and damp sand.

Typical habitats: Deciduous and evergreen hill forests, foothills, and wooded estates.

Field photography tip: Underside pale bands and tail length separate Common, Anomalous, and Blue Nawabs. Record both basking upperside and resting underside views, especially for bushbrowns, treebrowns, sailers, and oakleaf butterflies.

Identification marks

Upperwing: Males are rich fiery tawny-orange with a broad glossy black outer half on the forewing and wide black hindwing border enclosing two white-centered tornal dots; females are paler tawny-ochre crossed by a broad cream-white discal band edged inwardly in black, and bear longer twin hindwing tails than males.

Underwing: Purplish-brown to warm rufous-ochre glossed with satiny sheen and crisscrossed by fine white-edged zigzag black basal and discal lines.

Lookalikes: Separated from Black Rajah (Charaxes solon) by bright tawny-orange wing bases (rather than dark black-brown with narrow pale bands) and from Common Nawab by rich rufous-purple cryptic undersides.

Habitat & Tamil Nadu range

Habitats: Moist and dry deciduous hill forests, evergreen margins, shaded forest roads, and wooded valleys.

Tamil Nadu distribution: Western and Eastern Ghats hills and foothills including Mudumalai, Siruvani, Kallar, Sathyamangalam, Shevaroys, Kolli Hills, Sirumalai, and Kalakkad-Mundanthurai.

Seasonality: Seen throughout the year in forested tracts, most frequent from July to January and March-May.

Flight & behaviour

Territorial, fast-flying canopy dweller; males perch watchfully on sun-dappled branch tips and descend to feed on sap fluxes, rotting fruit, carrion, and wet mud.

Field note: South Indian populations belong to subspecies C. p. imna, long known to patrol high forest crowns before dropping rapidly onto decaying matter.
