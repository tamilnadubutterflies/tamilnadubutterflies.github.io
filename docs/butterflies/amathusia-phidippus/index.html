Field guide notes for Nymphalidae · Satyrinae (Browns, Bushbrowns, Treebrowns & Rings)

Family overview (Nymphalidae, Brush-footed butterflies): Tamil Nadu’s largest butterfly family (97 checklist species), encompassing tigers, crows, pansies, eggflies, sailers, barons, rajahs, browns, and Tamil Nadu’s state butterfly, the Tamil Yeoman.

Subfamily traits (Satyrinae): Shade-loving brown butterflies marked with concentric underside eyespots (ocelli) that frequently exhibit wet- and dry-season forms.

Typical habitats: Grasslands, forest understorey, bamboo groves, palm groves, and shaded gardens.

Field photography tip: Underside ocelli sizes, brand marks, and discal line shape (straight vs curved) are crucial for species ID. Record both basking upperside and resting underside views, especially for bushbrowns, treebrowns, sailers, and oakleaf butterflies.

Giant dusk-flying palm specialist bearing dual bullseye eyespots underneath

Identification marks

Upperwing: Massive warm chocolate-brown wings; males have a subtle hair tuft along the hindwing anal fold and short spatulate tornal lobes marked by two small black spots, while larger females are paler brown with an indistinct ochreous subapical band.

Underwing: Pale fawn-grey neatly striped with parallel straight brown and creamy bands, featuring two large concentric black-and-ochre eyespots on each hindwing (one near costa, one near tornus).

Lookalikes: Separated from Southern Duffer (Discophora lepida) by larger wingspan, parallel straight underside bands, spatulate tornal lobes, and giant dual hindwing ocelli.

Habitat & Tamil Nadu range

Habitats: Humid coconut and palmyra groves, shaded palm understories, and lowland evergreen edges.

Tamil Nadu distribution: Extremely localized in humid southern Western Ghats foothills and palm tracts of Kanyakumari and Kalakkad-Mundanthurai along the Tamil Nadu-Kerala border.

Seasonality: Sporadic and very rare, with records mostly around monsoon and post-monsoon months (August-December).

Flight & behaviour

Strictly crepuscular and shade-bound, resting immobile on palm trunks by day and flying low with heavy wingbeats at twilight to feed on fallen fruit and sap.

Field note: Peninsular Indian populations are extraordinarily rare and fragmented compared to Southeast Asian colonies, drawing recent taxonomic scrutiny.
